Chapter 2

The Crew Gathers

I didn’t sleep much. The map stayed on the milk crate next to my bed like it might slide off and disappear if I looked away too long. Every time I closed my eyes I saw my father’s careful letters on the back—This must be protected—and the thin groove the pen had left in the paper. By the time the sky over the bridge started turning that early gray, the garage was the only place that felt steady enough to think in. I went back.

The alley still held yesterday’s heat. I unlocked the side door and stepped into the closed-up air that always hit the same way: motor oil, old vinyl, the faint sweetness of dried café, and the ghost of plantains that never quite left. The Dominican flag still hung crooked on the far wall. The half-finished shelf for my mother’s santos waited in the same spot. I left the roll-up door down and set the map on the workbench under the dusty light. The record it had come from—an old 45 of Puerto Plata’s “Casita de Campo”—still sat beside it in its worn sleeve.

Lola showed up first. She came in carrying a paper bag with two buttered rolls from the bodega and that half-taken-apart pager still clipped to her belt loop. The screwdriver was behind her ear again. She didn’t ask what I’d decided. She just handed me a roll, sat on the edge of the workbench, and waited while the neighborhood woke up outside—someone calling up to a third-floor window, a car stereo already thumping, the metallic rattle of the 1 train somewhere under Broadway.

Carlos came next. My cousin from Highbridge, eighteen and already walking like the sidewalks had to make room for him. He had a black notebook under one arm and the sharp smell of spray paint still on his jacket. We’ve been tight since we were kids—closer than cousins, more like brothers who just happened to live on different sides of the river. When I told him there was something he needed to see, he only said, “Aight,” and leaned against the shelf with the coffee cans full of screws. He looked at the map the way he looked at a wall he was about to hit—measuring it, already seeing the lines underneath.

Ana arrived last. She lived in Queens, but last summer she worked at the Fort Washington Library through Alianza Dominicana’s youth employment program, mostly in the archival collections. That was how we’d started talking—her handling the older files and oral-history tapes, me killing time in the quiet after school. Seventeen, quiet in the way people get when they’re used to listening harder than they talk. Cuban on one side, Salvadoran on the other. She still carried the same worn composition book and a ballpoint that had already leaked once on the cover. When she stepped into the garage she took in the flag, the old El Diario clipping, the turntable, the dust in the amber light, and then she looked at me like she already understood this wasn’t a regular Saturday.
I spread the map flat. Nobody spoke for a while. Lola leaned in close enough that her hair almost brushed the paper. Carlos traced one of the older streets with a paint-stained finger. Ana’s hand hovered over the faded Spanish in the margins without touching it.
“This is your father’s writing,” Lola said. Not a question.

“Found it last night. Inside that record sleeve.” I nodded at the 45.

Carlos turned the map carefully. “These names don’t all match anymore. Some of these blocks got changed, or the buildings came down.”
Ana spoke softly, almost to the paper. “The style looks familiar. I’ve seen maps like this in the archival files at Fort Washington—old routes people used when they needed to move things quietly through the city. Letters. Money. Documents that couldn’t travel the regular way.” She glanced up at me. “Your father worked at the consulate.”

“Yeah.” The word came out flatter than I meant.

We stood there while the Heights kept going outside the door—kids already arguing over a basketball, the smell of frying oil drifting in from the alley, a bachata record starting up from an open window somewhere on the block. I told them the rest of it. The note. The way the map had been hidden like it was waiting. The feeling that the quiet in the garage had been holding its breath for this.
Lola picked up the record sleeve, turned it over, then looked at me. “You still have the record itself?”

I nodded and handed it to her. She slid the 45 out carefully and set it on the old turntable. The needle dropped with a soft crackle. Puerto Plata’s voice came through, warm and a little worn, singing about a little white house in the country—Casita de campo, tan blanca tan linda—a doorway, a valley under moonlight, a place that had sheltered a great love and now left the singer alone and asking when he would dream there again.

Nobody spoke while it played. When the song ended, Ana was the one who said it.

“Casita de campo,” she repeated quietly. “Little house in the country. There’s only one of those left up here.”

Carlos looked up. “Dyckman Farmhouse. Broadway and 204th. That old Dutch place they turned into a museum.”

Lola was already nodding. “The map has a heavy mark near there. And the song was the thing wrapping the map. That’s not an accident.”
I felt the tight thing in my chest loosen just enough to let the next breath in. “So that’s the first stop.”

Carlos was the first to say the rest of it out loud. “So we following it or what?”

Lola was already turning the pager over in her hands, thinking about parts. “We’d have to be careful. If somebody’s still looking for whatever this protected, they’re not going to be friendly about it.”

Ana closed her composition book. “Whatever it is, it mattered enough for him to hide it inside that song and write that note. I can still get into the archival collections at Fort Washington without anybody looking twice—I know how the older files and the oral-history tapes are kept from last summer. I can dig without it seeming strange.”

I looked at the three of them—Lola with the screwdriver still behind her ear, Carlos with paint on his cuffs, Ana holding her pen like it belonged there—and felt the same tight thing in my chest from the night before, only this time it had company.
“I need to know,” I said. “Why he hid it. Why it mattered enough to write that note. And if the people who came after him are still out there.”

Nobody argued. That was the part that stayed with me. They just started talking about what we had and what we didn’t. Lola wanted better light and a way to bring out more of the faded writing. Carlos knew which side streets and rooftops still connected the way the old maps showed. Ana already had a quiet way into the archival collections at Fort Washington from her summer with Alianza. Before we left, Ana carefully copied the lyrics of “Casita de Campo” onto a blank page of her composition book and folded it inside the cover. “Just in case,” she said.

The garage, with its oil smell and the crooked flag and the half-finished shelf, became the place we would come back to.
By the time the light through the high windows had shifted from amber to plain afternoon, we had a first stop. The heavy mark on the map sat farther north, near Broadway and 204th—right by the old Dyckman Farmhouse, the last little country house left standing in this part of the city. If the map and the song were telling the truth together, something about that ground still mattered.
We locked the roll-up door behind us and stepped into the alley. The smell of tostones was already thick in the air. Somewhere a radio was playing, but it wasn’t Puerto Plata this time. I kept the map folded inside my jacket, close against my side, and the copied lyrics stayed in Ana’s book.

For the first time since the funeral, the day ahead felt like it belonged to something larger than the quiet I’d been carrying around. We started walking.
