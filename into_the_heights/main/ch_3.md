Chapter 3

Casita de Campo

We walked north on Broadway like it was any other Saturday. The map stayed folded in my jacket. Ana carried the copied lyrics in her composition book. Carlos kept pace with that easy Highbridge stride, black notebook under his arm.

Past the 34th Precinct the sidewalks started to feel more open. Fort Tryon Park rose on the left, green and quiet above the traffic. We passed Anne Loftus Playground, kids already on the equipment, then the blocks began to tighten again as we got closer to Dyckman. Sidewalk vendors had their tables out—mangoes and avocados, bottles of cologne and little soaps still in the packaging. The smell of Dominican bakeries hit first, thick and sweet, the kind that makes you think of cake even if you’re not hungry. Cars sat double-parked with the windows down, dembow and old merengue competing from different stereos. People stood in front of restaurants talking with their hands. It felt like the neighborhood was doing what it always did—living out loud.

The Dyckman Farmhouse sat at the corner of Broadway and 204th like it had been left behind on purpose. White clapboard, low and square, a little park of grass and trees around it. The last casita de campo left standing in this part of the city. We stopped on the sidewalk and just looked at it for a minute. The song had brought us here. The map had marked it. Now we had to see what it wanted to show us.

We went in.

The museum was quiet, almost empty. Wide floorboards that had settled over two hundred years. Low ceilings. Rooms set up the way someone thought a Dutch farmhouse should look—parlors with old chairs, a bedroom, the winter kitchen down in the basement where the air felt cooler. Ana moved through it like she’d been here before. She didn’t raise her voice, but she started explaining things the way she did when she got interested—how the house had been rebuilt after the Revolutionary War, how the Dyckmans had farmed hundreds of acres up here when this part of Manhattan was still countryside, how the Hessian soldiers had lived in little huts on the land during the British occupation.

I watched her do it. It came out of her natural, like she couldn’t help connecting the dots out loud. Carlos caught my eye once and gave the smallest shake of his head, the way he did whenever Ana started turning a place into a lesson. He wasn’t mean about it. He just preferred the streets to stay streets.

We went back outside and walked the grounds. Brick paths, a small garden, the reproduction smokehouse standing off to one side. Under the trees in the back was the Hessian Hut—low, rough, reconstructed from the real remains someone had dug up years ago. Lola ran her hand along the logs. Carlos looked inside the dark doorway and didn’t say much. Ana kept talking, quieter now, about how many layers of people had moved across this same ground long before any of us were born.
It was Lola who stopped us near the back of the house.

She was looking at a stretch of exterior wall where the boards met the foundation. “That doesn’t sit right,” she said, low. The seam was almost invisible unless you were looking for something that didn’t belong—like a piece had been cut and set back a long time ago and never made perfect.
Carlos came over. Ana too. Lola pressed gently along the edge. There was a soft give, more like old wood shifting than a real latch, and a narrow panel moved. Behind it was a shallow space, no bigger than a shoebox turned on its side. Inside, folded once, was a single sheet of paper.
We took it out into the light.

The handwriting was careful, the same kind of careful my father used. The note was short:

Where the little white house still stands,
look for the light that only shows at night.
Not the streetlamp, not the moon—
the one that waits in the valley,
bathed in silver under a sky of stars.
Speak the lines the song already knows,
and the hidden road will answer.

Ana read it twice under her breath. Carlos leaned in, mouthing the words like he was listening for the bar structure. I watched them work it the same way I’d watched Carlos break down a verse on the train—finding the pattern, the repeated images, the thing that had to line up.

“Valley… light… moonlight… stars,” Ana said. She opened her composition book to the page where she’d copied the lyrics. Her finger stopped on the stanza.

“There in the valley
A light, bathed
By full moonlight
Under the palm trees
With a sky full of stars.”

Carlos nodded once. “That’s it. The song already said it.”

The riddle didn’t give us the next place. It told us the next mark on the map would only show under a certain kind of light. Lola looked at me. “Garage,” she said. “I’ve got what we need there.”
We stayed long enough to make sure we had the note. Ana copied it into her book. I folded the map again and put both papers in my jacket. Nobody said much. The quiet of the old house and the little park around it felt different now, like it had given us what it was supposed to give and was ready for us to leave.

Outside, the Dyckman noise came back all at once—car stereos, vendors calling out prices, the sweet bakery smell still hanging in the air. We stepped off the little park path and onto the sidewalk.
That was when I felt it.

A man standing across the street, near the corner, not moving with the rest of the block. Dark jacket, still posture, eyes that had already finished looking at us and were now just waiting. He didn’t call out. He didn’t follow. He just watched as we turned south, the way you watch something you plan to remember later.

Carlos noticed too. He didn’t say the name, but I saw the way his shoulders set.

El Jefe.

We kept walking. The map still had secrets on it. The song had done its part. And for the first time since we left the garage that morning, the quiet I’d been carrying started to feel like it had company of a different kind.

Behind us the white farmhouse sat in its little park, the last casita de campo left in the neighborhood, holding whatever else it still knew.
